@chapter 3 | Latitudes, Longitudes, Tropics and Seasons

@intro
This is the **map-reading and time** chapter. You need to know: 15° of longitude = **1 hour**, so 1° = **4 minutes**; time **gains** going east and **loses** going west; the **International Date Line** at 180°; which countries the **Equator** and the **Tropics** cross; and what happens at the **solstices and equinoxes**. Most of these questions can be answered with one line of arithmetic. It contains **{pyqs} PYQs and {ones} one-liners**.
@end

@section 3.1 | Latitudes, Longitudes and Time

@pyq U.P. Lower Sub. (Pre) 2009
Point of intersection of the Equator and the Prime Meridian is located at
(a) Atlantic Ocean
(b) Ghana
(c) Morocco
(d) Pacefic Ocean
@ans (a)
@end

The point 0°, 0° lies in the **Gulf of Guinea** (Atlantic Ocean), south of Ghana. It is sometimes jokingly called "**Null Island**".

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Mains) 2011

(a) Beijing
(b) New Delhi
(c) New York
(d) Rome
@ans (d)

Latitudes: **Rome about 41.9°N**, New York about 40.7°N, Beijing about 39.9°N, New Delhi about 28.6°N. European cities lie much farther north than their mild climate suggests, thanks to the North Atlantic Drift.

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 2006
The local time of a place is 6:00 AM when the Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) is 3:00 AM, what is the longitude of the place ?
(a) 45o West
(b) 45° East
(c) 120° East
(d) 120° West
@ans (b)
@end

@table
Time rule | Working
360° = 24 hours | So 15° = 1 hour and 1° = 4 minutes
Going east of Greenwich | Local time is **ahead** (add 4 min per degree)
Going west of Greenwich | Local time is **behind** (subtract 4 min per degree)
Example: GMT 12:00, local 17:00 | 5 h × 15° = **75° E**
Example: GMT 03:00, local 06:00 | 3 h × 15° = **45° E**
Indian Standard Time | 82°30' E (Mirzapur, UP), so GMT + 5 h 30 min
@end

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 2000
Which one of the following longitudes along with the Prime Meridian forms a great circle on the globe.
(a) 0o
(b) 90o E
(c) 90o W
(d) 180o
@ans (d)
@end

A **great circle** divides the globe into two equal halves. Any meridian together with its opposite meridian forms one. The **Equator** is the only parallel that is a great circle. The shortest route between two points on a sphere follows a great circle, which is why long-haul flights take polar routes.

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 2001
In the New Millennium, the first ray of the Sun was seen in which of the following Meridians of India –
(a) 2o 30'W
(b) 82o 30'E
(c) 92o 30'W
(d) 93o 30'E
@ans (d)
@end

The first sunrise of the new millennium in India was seen on **Katchal Island** (Nicobar), at about 93°30'E. On the mainland, **Dong** in Arunachal Pradesh sees the first sunrise.

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 1997
There is no minimum distance between two places on the globe :
(a) 45o N. Lat
(b) 45o S. Lat
(c) Prime Meridian
(d) International Date Line
@ans (d)
@end

The **International Date Line** mostly follows 180° but **zigzags** around land (near the Bering Strait, the Aleutians, and the Kiribati-Samoa-Tonga region) so that no country is split between two dates. Crossing it **westward adds a day**, and crossing it **eastward subtracts a day**.

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 2000
Captain of a ship moving Westward records the local time of 10-00 hrs Monday at 90o W. If the speed of the ship is the same as the speed of the rotation of the earth, which of the following local time and day would be found by the captain at International date line:
(a) 04.00 Monday
(b) 10.00 Monday
(c) 10.00 Tuesday
(d) 16.00 Tuesday
@ans (b)
@end

A ship sailing west as fast as the Earth turns east keeps the Sun at the same point in its sky, so local time on board stays at **10:00**. It reaches the date line still on **Monday** (just before it would change to Tuesday on crossing).

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 1997
At the Winter Solstice the noon altitude of the Sun at the Tropic of Cancer is :
(a) 23.5o
(b) 43.0o
(c) 47.0o
(d) 66.5o
@ans (b)
@end

On **22 December** the Sun is overhead at the **Tropic of Capricorn** (23.5°S). The Tropic of Cancer is 47° away, so the noon Sun there stands at **90° − 47° = 43°**.

@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) 1990

@end

@table
Date (approx.) | Event | Sun overhead | Northern Hemisphere
21 March | Vernal (spring) equinox | Equator | Day = night everywhere
21 June | Summer solstice | Tropic of Cancer | Longest day; shortest noon shadow; midnight sun north of Arctic Circle
23 September | Autumnal equinox | Equator | Day = night everywhere
22 December | Winter solstice | Tropic of Capricorn | Shortest day; midnight sun south of Antarctic Circle
3-4 January | Perihelion | - | Earth nearest the Sun (yet it is winter in the north)
@end

**Norway** is called the "Land of the Midnight Sun". The poles have about **six months** of daylight and six months of night.

@pattern
About half of these questions can be **calculated** (time from longitude, Sun's altitude) and half are **lists** (countries crossed by the Equator or the Tropics, solstice dates). Practise the 4-minutes-per-degree rule until it is automatic. In the country-list questions, the odd one out is usually a neighbour just off the line, such as Iran, Bolivia or Rwanda.
@end
