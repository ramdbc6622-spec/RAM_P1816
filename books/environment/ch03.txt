@pyq U.P.P.C.S. (Mains) 2013
Who among the following had used the term 'Biodiversity' for the first time?
(a) C.J. Barrow
(b) De Carter
(c) Walter G. Rosen
(d) D.R. Batish
@ans (c)
@end

**Walter G. Rosen** coined "biodiversity" in **1985**. **E.O. Wilson** made it popular. Biodiversity has three levels: **genetic** (within a species), **species** and **ecosystem** diversity. Whittaker's measures are **alpha** (within a community), **beta** (between communities) and **gamma** (across a whole region).

**2011-2020** was the UN **Decade on Biodiversity** (the **Aichi Targets**), and **2021-2030** is the UN **Decade on Ecosystem Restoration**. The Aichi Targets were followed in **December 2022** by the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework** (CBD COP15), whose headline goal is "**30 by 30**": protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030.

**Silent Valley** (Palakkad, Kerala) has an untouched tropical evergreen forest. It was saved from a hydroelectric dam by the "**Save Silent Valley**" movement and became a national park in **1984**. The **Valley of Flowers** (Chamoli, Uttarakhand) and **Nanda Devi** together form a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

@table
Biodiversity hotspot (India's share) | Coverage
**Himalaya** | Indian Himalaya, Nepal, Bhutan
**Indo-Burma** | North-east India (except Assam plains), Myanmar, Thailand, Indo-China
**Western Ghats and Sri Lanka** | The Western Ghats; the richest region for endemic plants and amphibians
**Sundaland** | **Nicobar Islands** (with Indonesia, Malaysia)
@end

**Norman Myers** proposed the idea of hotspots (1988). **Conservation International** now lists **36** hotspots worldwide. To qualify, an area must have at least **1,500 endemic vascular plants** and must have lost at least **70%** of its original habitat. India is one of the 17 **megadiverse** countries, with about 2.4% of the world's land and about 7-8% of its recorded species.

The main threats can be remembered as "**HIPPO**": **H**abitat loss (the biggest), **I**nvasive species, **P**ollution, human **P**opulation and **O**ver-exploitation, with **climate change** now added as a fast-growing sixth. Examples of invasive species in India include **Lantana**, **Parthenium** (congress grass), **water hyacinth** and **Prosopis juliflora**.

@one U.P.P.C.S. (Pre) (Re-Exam) 2015
Which one is the most significant factor in coral bleaching?
@ans Global warming
@end

Corals live with tiny algae called **zooxanthellae**, which give them food and colour. When the sea stays about **1 °C or more above** its usual summer maximum, the corals expel the algae and turn white. This is **bleaching**. The **fourth global bleaching event** (2023-25) affected more than 80% of the world's reefs. India's reef areas are the Gulf of Mannar, the Gulf of Kutch, Lakshadweep and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

@table
In-situ (in the natural habitat) | Ex-situ (away from the natural habitat)
Biosphere reserves | Botanical gardens, zoos, aquaria
National parks, wildlife sanctuaries | **Seed banks** (e.g. Svalbard Global Seed Vault, Norway)
Conservation and community reserves | **Gene / cryo banks**: material stored in **liquid nitrogen** at **-196 °C**
**Sacred groves** (e.g. Khasi, Garo hills; Kerala's *kavu*) | Tissue culture, captive breeding
Ramsar sites | **NBPGR** gene bank (New Delhi); **LaCONES** (Hyderabad) for endangered animals
@end

@trap
**Habitat destruction** is always the main cause of loss, ahead of pollution, invasive species or over-exploitation. **Botanical gardens and zoos are ex-situ**, while **sacred groves are in-situ**. Cryo banks use **liquid nitrogen**, not CO2. The **Vindhyas** are not a hotspot. India's four hotspots are the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar).
@end
