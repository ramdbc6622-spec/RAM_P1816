## Article 124(1) — Composition of the Supreme Court

### Constitutional Text

"There shall be a Supreme Court of India consisting of a Chief Justice of India and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, of not more than seven other Judges."

### Clause-by-Clause Analysis

**"There shall be a Supreme Court of India"** — The SC is a constitutional body, not a statutory creation. Its existence is guaranteed by the Constitution itself. Parliament cannot abolish it. Compare with tribunals under Art 323A/323B, which are statutory creations and can be abolished by Parliament.

**"consisting of a Chief Justice of India"** — The CJI is constitutionally mandated. There must always be a CJI. The office cannot be abolished or kept vacant indefinitely. When the CJI is absent or the office is vacant, Art 126 provides for an Acting CJI appointed by the President.

**"and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number"** — This is the enabling clause that allows Parliament to increase the number of judges beyond seven. The Constitution deliberately left the door open for expansion. Key points:

- Only **Parliament** can change the number — not the President, not the CJI, not the executive by order.
- The change must be **by law** — meaning a statute passed through the regular legislative process (Bills in both Houses + Presidential assent).
- Parliament can only prescribe a **larger** number — the Constitution does not expressly authorize Parliament to reduce the number below seven.

**"of not more than seven other Judges"** — This is the original 1950 number. The Supreme Court began with CJI + 7 = 8 judges. This number has been increased multiple times through amendments to the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956.

### The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 — Amendment History

| Year | Amendment | Strength (excl. CJI) | Total (incl. CJI) | Enabling Legislation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1950 | Original Constitution | 7 | 8 | Art 124(1) itself |
| 1956 | Act enacted | 10 | 11 | SC (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 |
| 1960 | 1st Amendment | 13 | 14 | Amendment Act, 1960 |
| 1977 | 2nd Amendment | 17 | 18 | Amendment Act, 1977 |
| 1986 | 3rd Amendment | 25 | 26 | Amendment Act, 1986 |
| 2008 | 4th Amendment | 30 | 31 | Amendment Act, 2008 |
| 2019 | 5th Amendment | 33 | 34 | Amendment Act, 2019 |
| 2026 | 6th Amendment | 37 | 38 | Amendment Act, 2026 |

**2026 Amendment — Key Facts:**

- Cabinet approved on 5 May 2026.
- President promulgated ordinance on 16 May 2026 under Art 123.
- Lok Sabha passed the Bill.
- Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 5 August 2026.
- President's assent received on 11 August 2026.
- Reason: over 92,000 cases pending before SC as of 1 January 2026.
- Increase: +4 judges (from 33 to 37, excluding CJI; from 34 to 38 total).

### Cross-Connections

**Art 124(1) → Art 2 of the 1956 Act:** Art 124(1) is the constitutional parent; the 1956 Act is the implementing statute. The Constitution sets the floor (seven); the Act raises the ceiling.

**Art 124(1) → Art 145(3):** The minimum bench for deciding a substantial question of constitutional interpretation is 5 judges. As the total strength increases, the Court can constitute more Constitution Benches simultaneously.

**Art 124(1) → Art 146(1):** Appointments of officers and servants are made by the CJI — but the number of judges affects the administrative structure, number of benches, and staffing requirements.

**Art 124(1) → Seventh Schedule, Union List Entry 77:** "Constitution, organisation, jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court" is a Union subject. Only Parliament can legislate on it.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "The Constitution fixes the strength of the Supreme Court at 34/38." — **FALSE.** The Constitution says "seven." Parliament increases it by law. The number 38 comes from the 1956 Act as amended, not from the Constitution.

**Trap 2:** "The President can increase the number of SC judges by executive order." — **FALSE.** Only Parliament by law. The 2026 ordinance was a temporary measure under Art 123 until Parliament passed the Act.

**Trap 3:** "The strength has always been 34 since 2019." — **OUTDATED.** It was raised to 38 in 2026.

**Trap 4:** "The CJI is in addition to the prescribed number." — This needs careful reading. The original text says CJI + seven others = 8 total. The 1956 Act prescribes the number of "other Judges" (currently 37). CJI is always in addition to that number. Total = CJI + others = 1 + 37 = 38.

## Article 124(2) — Appointment, Tenure & Resignation/Removal of SC Judges

### Constitutional Text

"Every Judge of the Supreme Court shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal and shall hold office until he attains the age of sixty-five years:

Provided that — (a) a Judge may, by writing under his hand addressed to the President, resign his office; (b) a Judge may be removed from his office in the manner provided in clause (4)."

### Clause-by-Clause Analysis

**"appointed by the President"** — The formal appointing authority is the President. Not the Prime Minister, not Parliament, not the CJI. The President acts on advice — but whose advice? This is where the entire Collegium controversy sits.

**"by warrant under his hand and seal"** — This is a specific constitutional formality. The appointment is made through a presidential warrant — a formal written instrument bearing the President's signature and the seal of India. This is not a gazette notification or a cabinet resolution. The phrase "under his hand and seal" appears in the Constitution only for specific high-constitutional appointments (SC/HC judges, Governors, etc.).

**"shall hold office until he attains the age of sixty-five years"** — This is a mandatory retirement age. Not optional, not extendable. A SC judge's tenure ends automatically on turning 65. No extension, no re-appointment.

**Compare with other constitutional functionaries:**

| Functionary | Retirement Age | Appointment By |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SC Judge | 65 years | President (warrant under hand and seal) |
| HC Judge | 62 years | President (warrant under hand and seal) |
| CAG | 65 years | President (warrant under hand and seal) |
| CEC/EC | 65 years | President |
| UPSC Chairman | 65 years | President |
| Attorney General | No fixed age (holds during pleasure) | President |

**Proviso (a) — Resignation:** A Judge may resign by writing under his hand addressed to the President. The resignation takes effect when accepted by the President (convention, not expressly stated). The resignation is a voluntary act. The writing must be by the Judge's own hand — not through a representative.

**Proviso (b) — Removal:** Cross-reference to Art 124(4). Removal is through a specific parliamentary process (impeachment-like). This is the only involuntary method of ending a SC judge's tenure before age 65.

### The Consultation/Appointment Controversy — Full History

The original Art 124(2) said the President shall appoint judges "after consultation with such of the Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary for the purpose" and added that the CJI shall always be consulted for non-CJI appointments.

This was changed by three landmark cases and one failed constitutional amendment:

**1. First Judges Case — S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)**

- The Supreme Court held that "consultation" does not mean "concurrence."
- The executive (President on Council of Ministers' advice) had the final say in appointments.
- CJI's opinion was important but not binding.
- This gave primacy to the executive in judicial appointments.

**2. Second Judges Case — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)**

- A 9-judge bench overruled the First Judges Case.
- Held that "consultation" with the CJI means "concurrence" — CJI's recommendation is binding.
- Established the **Collegium system**: CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges for SC appointments.
- Shifted primacy from the executive to the judiciary.
- This is a judicial creation — the word "Collegium" does not appear in the Constitution.

**3. Third Judges Case — In re Presidential Reference (1998)**

- The President made a reference to the SC under Art 143 seeking clarification on the Second Judges Case.
- The SC expanded the Collegium: CJI + **4** senior-most SC judges for SC appointments.
- For HC appointments: CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges + consultation with senior HC judges of the concerned HC.
- Established the **memorandum of procedure** for appointments.

**4. NJAC — 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014) and its Striking Down (2015)**

- Parliament enacted the Constitution (Ninety-ninth Amendment) Act, 2014 to replace the Collegium with the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC).
- Inserted Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C.
- The NJAC Act, 2014 was also passed.
- In **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)**, a 5-judge Constitution Bench (4:1) struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act.
- Ground: The NJAC violated the **basic structure** of the Constitution — specifically, the independence of the judiciary.
- Justice J. Chelameswar was the lone dissenter.
- Result: The Collegium system was restored and continues to operate.

### Current Position (as of 2026)

- **Formal appointment:** By the President under Art 124(2).
- **Effective recommendation:** By the Collegium (CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges) — per the Second and Third Judges Cases.
- **Constitutional text:** Still refers to NJAC (Art 124A-C are physically present in the Constitution text) — but these provisions are **inoperative** because they were struck down.
- **The Collegium is not mentioned in the Constitution.** It is entirely a judicial creation from the Second and Third Judges Cases.

### Cross-Connections

**Art 124(2) → Art 217:** HC judges are also appointed by President by warrant, but with a different consultation process — CJI + Governor of the State + Chief Justice of the HC.

**Art 124(2) → Art 126:** When CJI is absent/vacant → President appoints Acting CJI. This is a different mechanism from the regular appointment process.

**Art 124(2) → Art 127:** Ad hoc judge appointment has a different process — CJI initiates, President consents.

**Art 124(2) → Art 222:** Transfer of HC judges — President acts after consultation with CJI. Collegium recommends.

**Art 124(2) → Art 124(6):** After appointment, the judge must take oath before the President before entering office.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "The CJI appoints SC judges." — **FALSE.** The President appoints. The CJI (through the Collegium) recommends. The formal appointing authority is always the President.

**Trap 2:** "The Collegium system is provided for in the Constitution." — **FALSE.** The Constitution provides for "consultation." The Collegium is a judicial creation from the Second and Third Judges Cases. It has no constitutional text backing it.

**Trap 3:** "NJAC is the current system of judicial appointments." — **FALSE.** NJAC was struck down in 2015. The Collegium system was restored.

**Trap 4:** "SC judges retire at 62 years." — **FALSE.** That is the HC retirement age. SC = 65.

**Trap 5:** "A SC judge can resign by writing to the CJI." — **FALSE.** The resignation is addressed to the **President**, not the CJI.

**Trap 6:** "The Collegium consists of CJI + 2 judges." — **PARTIALLY OUTDATED.** The Second Judges Case (1993) said CJI + 2. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded it to CJI + 4 for SC appointments. Current Collegium = CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges.

## Article 124(2A) — Determination of Age of SC Judges

### Constitutional Text

"The age of a Judge of the Supreme Court shall be determined by such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law provide."

### Analysis

This clause was inserted by the **Constitution (Fifteenth Amendment) Act, 1963**. Before this, there was no express provision for resolving disputes about a SC judge's age. Since tenure ends at 65 (Art 124(2)), the question "how old is the judge?" becomes legally critical near retirement.

**Key points:**

- Parliament determines the **authority** (who decides) and the **manner** (how they decide).
- This is a delegation to Parliament — until Parliament legislates, there is no statutory mechanism. In practice, the date of birth recorded at the time of appointment is treated as conclusive.
- Compare with Art 217(3) for HC judges — identical provision: age determined by authority and manner prescribed by Parliament.

### MCQ

**MCQ 1:** Under Article 124(2A), the age of a Judge of the Supreme Court is determined by:

(a) The Chief Justice of India (b) The President (c) Such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law provide (d) The Registrar of the Supreme Court based on birth certificate

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 124(2A) expressly delegates this to Parliament. It was inserted by the 15th Amendment Act, 1963.

### Revision Point

- Art 124(2A): Age dispute → Parliament prescribes authority + manner. Inserted by 15th Amendment, 1963.

## Article 124(3) — Qualifications for Appointment as SC Judge

### Constitutional Text

"A person shall not be qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court unless he is a citizen of India and — (a) has been for at least five years a Judge of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession; or (b) has been for at least ten years an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession; or (c) is, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist."

**Explanation I:** "High Court" means a High Court which exercises, or which at any time before the commencement of this Constitution exercised, jurisdiction in any part of the territory of India.

**Explanation II:** In computing the 10-year period for an advocate, any period during which a person has held judicial office not inferior to that of a district judge after he became an advocate shall be included.

### Detailed Analysis

**Mandatory prerequisite — Citizenship:** A person must be a citizen of India. This is a non-negotiable threshold. No foreign national, no OCI holder, no person who has renounced Indian citizenship can be appointed.

**Route (a) — The Judge Route:**

- Minimum **5 years** as a Judge of a **High Court** (not Supreme Court, not District Court).
- Can be 5 years across two or more High Courts **in succession** — meaning continuous service without a break. If a judge served in Delhi HC for 3 years, then transferred to Bombay HC for 2 years, the total 5 years counts.
- "High Court" per Explanation I includes pre-Constitution High Courts — this was relevant in the early years after 1950.

**Route (b) — The Advocate Route:**

- Minimum **10 years** as an advocate of a **High Court** — NOT of the Supreme Court.
- This is a critical point that examiners exploit. The text says "advocate of a High Court." A person who has practiced only in the Supreme Court or only in District Courts does not qualify under this route.
- Can be 10 years across two or more High Courts in succession.
- **Explanation II** is important: if an advocate became a District Judge (or higher judicial officer) after becoming an advocate, that judicial service period is ADDED to the advocacy period. Example: A person was an advocate for 6 years, then became a District Judge for 5 years → total counted = 6 + 5 = 11 years (qualifies). But the judicial office must be "not inferior to that of a district judge" — so service as a Munsif or Sub-Judge does NOT count.

**Route (c) — The Distinguished Jurist Route:**

- "In the opinion of the President" — this is a subjective assessment. There is no statutory definition of "distinguished jurist."
- A distinguished jurist is typically a legal scholar, academic, or person of exceptional standing in law — NOT necessarily a practicing advocate or sitting judge.
- This route has **never been used** to appoint a SC judge as of 2026. It exists as a constitutional possibility but remains unused in practice.
- This route is available **only for SC judges** — there is no equivalent for HC judges under Art 217.

### Cross-Comparison: SC vs HC Judge Qualifications

| Parameter | SC Judge (Art 124(3)) | HC Judge (Art 217(2)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citizenship | Must be citizen of India | Must be citizen of India |
| Judge route | 5 years as HC judge | — (no judge route) |
| Advocate route | 10 years as HC advocate | 10 years as HC advocate |
| Distinguished jurist | Yes — in President's opinion | NOT available |
| Judicial service route | — | 10 years in judicial service in India |
| Minimum age | No minimum prescribed | No minimum prescribed |
| Maximum age at appointment | No maximum prescribed (but retires at 65) | No maximum prescribed (but retires at 62) |

**Key differences to memorize:**

1. **Distinguished jurist** route exists ONLY for SC — not for HC.
2. **Judicial service** route (10 years holding judicial office in India) exists ONLY for HC — not for SC.
3. SC requires 5 years as HC **judge**; HC has no such requirement.
4. Both require 10 years as HC **advocate** — this is common.
5. Neither has a minimum age requirement — but in practice, the 5/10-year experience requirements create a de facto minimum age.

### Cross-Connections

**Art 124(3) → Art 217(2):** The comparison table above. This is the single most frequently tested comparison in judiciary-related MCQs.

**Art 124(3) → Art 124(2):** Qualifications determine eligibility; Art 124(2) determines the appointment process.

**Art 124(3) → Art 128:** A retired HC judge can sit in the SC under Art 128 only if "duly qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court" — meaning they must satisfy Art 124(3).

**Art 124(3) → Art 127:** An ad hoc HC judge must also be "duly qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court."

**Art 124(3) → Explanation II:** The district-judge-service-counting provision has a practical impact: many SC judges come through a career path of advocate → district judge → HC judge → SC judge. Explanation II ensures their total service is counted correctly.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "10 years as an advocate of the Supreme Court" — **WRONG.** The text says "advocate of a **High Court**." SC practice alone does not qualify.

**Trap 2:** "Distinguished jurist route has been used for X person" — **LIKELY FALSE.** As of 2026, this route has never been used for any SC appointment.

**Trap 3:** "Distinguished jurist is also a qualification for HC judges" — **FALSE.** Art 217(2) does not include this route. It is exclusive to SC.

**Trap 4:** "5 years as a District Judge qualifies for SC appointment" — **FALSE.** Route (a) requires 5 years as a **High Court judge**, not district judge. District judge service counts under Explanation II only for computing the advocate-period in route (b).

**Trap 5:** "There is a minimum age for SC judges" — **FALSE.** No minimum age is prescribed. But practical experience requirements (5 or 10 years) create a de facto minimum.

**Trap 6:** "A person can qualify under both route (a) and route (b) simultaneously" — This is actually **possible and not a trap**. A person who was an advocate for 10 years, then became an HC judge for 5 years, qualifies under both routes. The routes are alternatives ("or"), not mutually exclusive.

## Article 124(4)–(5) — Removal of SC Judges (Impeachment)

### Constitutional Text — Clause (4)

"A Judge of the Supreme Court shall not be removed from his office except by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting has been presented to the President in the same session for such removal on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity."

### Constitutional Text — Clause (5)

"Parliament may by law regulate the procedure for the presentation of an address and for the investigation and proof of the misbehaviour or incapacity of a Judge under clause (4)."

### Detailed Breakdown of Clause (4)

**Step 1 — Address by Each House:** Both Lok Sabha AND Rajya Sabha must separately pass an address (motion) for removal. Not just one House — BOTH.

**Step 2 — Double Majority in EACH House:** The address must be supported by:

| Requirement | What It Means |
| --- | --- |
| Majority of total membership of that House | More than half of ALL members of the House (including vacant seats and absentees). For Lok Sabha (545): more than 272. For Rajya Sabha (245): more than 122. |
| Not less than two-thirds of members present and voting | At least 2/3 of those who are actually present and participating in the vote. |

BOTH conditions must be satisfied SIMULTANEOUSLY in EACH House. This is called a **special majority** or **effective majority** — the same type used for constitutional amendments under Art 368.

**Step 3 — Same Session:** The address must be presented to the President in the **same session**. This means both Houses must pass the motion within one session of Parliament. If one House passes it in the Monsoon Session and the other in the Winter Session, it does not count. Both must act in the same session.

**Step 4 — President's Order:** After receiving the address from both Houses, the President passes an order removing the judge. The President acts on the address — this is not a discretionary power. The President cannot refuse to act once both Houses have presented the address.

**Step 5 — Grounds:** Only TWO grounds for removal:

1. **Proved misbehaviour** — Not merely alleged misbehaviour. The misbehaviour must be investigated and proved before the address is passed.
2. **Incapacity** — Physical or mental incapacity that renders the judge unable to perform duties.

No other ground is valid — not "incompetence," not "inefficiency," not "corruption" (unless proved as misbehaviour), not "poor judgment."

### The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

Art 124(5) authorizes Parliament to regulate the procedure. Parliament enacted the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** for this purpose.

**Key provisions of the 1968 Act:**

- A removal motion can be initiated by:
  - **Lok Sabha:** Motion signed by not less than **100 members**
  - **Rajya Sabha:** Motion signed by not less than **50 members**
- The Speaker/Chairman may admit or refuse the motion.
- If admitted, an **Inquiry Committee** of 3 members is constituted:
  - A sitting SC judge (nominated by CJI)
  - A sitting HC Chief Justice (nominated by CJI)
  - A distinguished jurist (nominated by Speaker/Chairman)
- The Committee investigates and submits a report.
- If the Committee finds the charges proved, the House debates and votes.
- The judge is entitled to appear and be heard during the inquiry.

### Historical Attempts at Removal

**1. Justice V. Ramaswami (1993):**

- First-ever impeachment motion against a SC judge.
- Motion was admitted in Lok Sabha.
- Inquiry Committee found charges of misbehaviour proved.
- But the motion failed in Lok Sabha — it did not get the required majority (Congress abstained).
- Result: No SC judge has ever been removed by impeachment.

**2. Justice Soumitra Sen (2011):**

- This was a HC judge (Calcutta HC), not SC — but the process is the same (Art 217(1)(b) read with Art 124(4)).
- Rajya Sabha passed the motion.
- Before Lok Sabha could vote, Justice Sen resigned.
- Resignation does not stop the proceedings legally, but it rendered the motion infructuous in practice.

**3. CJI Dipak Misra (2018):**

- Impeachment motion filed by opposition MPs in Rajya Sabha.
- Rajya Sabha Chairman Venkaiah Naidu rejected the motion.
- Motion was not admitted — it never reached the inquiry stage.

**Key takeaway: No SC or HC judge has ever been successfully removed through the impeachment process in India.**

### Comparison — Removal of Constitutional Functionaries

| Functionary | Removed By | Majority Required | Ground | Article |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SC Judge | President after address by each House | Special majority (total + 2/3 P&V) | Proved misbehaviour or incapacity | 124(4) |
| HC Judge | Same as SC Judge | Same | Same | 217(1)(b) read with 124(4) |
| President | Impeachment by Parliament | 2/3 of total membership in each House | Violation of Constitution | 61 |
| CAG | Same as SC Judge | Same as SC Judge | Same grounds | 148(1) |
| CEC | Same as SC Judge | Same as SC Judge | Same grounds | 324(5) |
| UPSC Chairman | President — by order (after SC inquiry) | SC inquiry | Misbehaviour (proved by SC) | 317(1) |

### Cross-Connections

**Art 124(4) → Art 217(1)(b):** HC judges are removed by the SAME process — address by each House + Presidential order. The Constitution does not provide a separate removal mechanism for HC judges.

**Art 124(4) → Art 218:** Provisions of Art 124 relating to conditions of service apply to HC judges too — this cross-reference is how Art 124(4) becomes applicable to HC judges.

**Art 124(4) → Art 61:** Presidential impeachment uses a DIFFERENT majority — 2/3 of **total membership** (not 2/3 of present and voting + majority of total). Presidential impeachment also requires a different ground: "violation of the Constitution."

**Art 124(4) → Art 148(1):** The CAG can be removed only "in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court." This directly incorporates Art 124(4).

**Art 124(4) → Art 324(5):** The CEC can be removed only in like manner and on like grounds as a SC judge.

**Art 124(5) → Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968:** The implementing statute. The Act prescribes the procedure for the removal process — notice requirements, inquiry committee composition, hearing procedures.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "Two-thirds of total membership" — **WRONG.** The requirement is two-thirds of members **present and voting** PLUS majority of **total membership**. This is a double majority, not a single two-thirds majority.

**Trap 2:** "The President can remove a SC judge on his own" — **FALSE.** The President can only act AFTER receiving the address from both Houses.

**Trap 3:** "Incompetence is a ground for removal" — **FALSE.** The word is "incapacity," not "incompetence." Incapacity means inability to perform (physical/mental). Incompetence means poor quality of work — not a constitutional ground.

**Trap 4:** "A SC judge has been removed through impeachment" — **FALSE.** No SC or HC judge has ever been successfully removed in India.

**Trap 5:** "The motion can be passed across two sessions" — **FALSE.** Both Houses must present the address in the **same session**.

**Trap 6:** "50 members are needed to initiate the motion in Lok Sabha" — **FALSE.** Lok Sabha requires **100 members**. Rajya Sabha requires 50.

**Trap 7:** "The Inquiry Committee has 5 members" — **FALSE.** It has 3 members: one SC judge + one HC Chief Justice + one distinguished jurist.

## Article 124(6) — Oath of SC Judges

### Constitutional Text

"Every person appointed to be a Judge of the Supreme Court shall, before he enters upon his office, make and subscribe before the President, or some person appointed in that behalf by him, an oath or affirmation according to the form set out for the purpose in the Third Schedule."

### Analysis

- **Before whom:** The President of India — or a person appointed by the President for this purpose. In practice, the CJI often administers the oath to other SC judges on the President's behalf. The CJI himself takes the oath before the President.
- **When:** Before entering upon office — not after. A judge cannot begin working before taking the oath.
- **Form:** As set out in the **Third Schedule** of the Constitution.
- **Oath or affirmation:** Both options are available — "oath" for those who wish to swear, "affirmation" for those who prefer not to invoke a religious form.

**Comparison with HC judges:** Under Art 219, HC judges take oath before the **Governor of the State** (or someone appointed by the Governor). This is a tested comparison.

| Functionary | Oath Before | Schedule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SC Judge | President (or his appointee) | Third Schedule |
| HC Judge | Governor of the State | Third Schedule |
| President | CJI (or senior-most SC judge) | Art 60 |
| Governor | CJ of the HC (or senior-most HC judge) | Art 159 |

(a) The Chief Justice of India (b) The President of India or a person appointed by the President (c) The Speaker of Lok Sabha (d) The Governor of the State from which the Judge hails

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 124(6). The CJI takes oath before the President directly. Other SC judges typically take oath before the CJI as the President's appointee. HC judges take oath before the Governor (Art 219) — a common trap.

---

**MCQ 2:** The form of oath for a Supreme Court Judge is set out in:

(a) The First Schedule (b) The Second Schedule (c) The Third Schedule (d) The Seventh Schedule

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 124(6) — Third Schedule. The Second Schedule deals with salaries/emoluments. The First Schedule lists States and UTs. The Seventh Schedule lists Union/State/Concurrent Lists.

### Revision Points

1. SC Judge oath → before President or his appointee → Third Schedule.
2. HC Judge oath → before Governor → Third Schedule.
3. Oath must be taken BEFORE entering office.

## Article 124(7) — Post-Retirement Bar on Practice

### Constitutional Text

"No person who has held office as a Judge of the Supreme Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India."

### Analysis

This is an **absolute and total bar**. A retired SC judge cannot:

- Plead (argue) in any court — SC, HC, District Court, Tribunal, or any other court.
- Act (file, represent, appear) before any authority — judicial, quasi-judicial, or administrative.
- Practice anywhere within the territory of India — no exceptions, no permissions, no waivers.

The rationale is to prevent retired SC judges from using their influence and connections in courts where they were once the highest judicial authority. Since SC decisions bind ALL courts in India (Art 141), allowing a retired SC judge to practice would create an inherent imbalance.

### Comparison with HC Judges — Art 220

Art 220 provides: "No person who, after the commencement of this Constitution, has held office as a permanent Judge of a High Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority in India **except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts**."

This creates a **critical distinction:**

| Parameter | Retired SC Judge (Art 124(7)) | Retired HC Judge (Art 220) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can practice in SC? | NO | YES |
| Can practice in other HCs? | NO | YES (any HC except the one where they served) |
| Can practice in District Courts? | NO | NO |
| Can practice before tribunals? | NO | NO |
| Nature of bar | Absolute — no court, no authority, anywhere in India | Partial — cannot practice in courts/authorities EXCEPT SC and other HCs |

**The key exam point:** Retired SC judges have an ABSOLUTE bar. Retired HC judges have a PARTIAL bar — they can practice in the SC and in High Courts other than the one where they served.

### Cross-Connections

**Art 124(7) → Art 220:** The comparison above is a guaranteed MCQ source.

**Art 124(7) → Art 148(4):** The CAG, after ceasing to hold office, "shall not be eligible for further office either under the Government of India or under the Government of any State." Different kind of post-retirement bar — against government office, not legal practice.

**Art 124(7) → Post-retirement appointments:** The bar is on pleading/acting in courts. It does NOT bar a retired SC judge from being appointed to commissions, committees, tribunals (as chairperson/member), governorships, or diplomatic posts. This is why retired CJIs/SC judges have been appointed as Governors, Rajya Sabha members, chairpersons of NHRC, Law Commission, etc.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "A retired SC judge can practice in the Supreme Court with special permission" — **FALSE.** The bar is absolute. No permission can override Art 124(7).

**Trap 2:** "A retired HC judge cannot practice anywhere" — **FALSE.** Art 220 allows practice in SC and other HCs.

**Trap 3:** "A retired SC judge cannot be appointed as Governor" — **FALSE.** Art 124(7) bars **pleading/acting in courts**. It does not bar government/constitutional appointments.

**Trap 4:** "Art 220 applies to ad hoc or additional HC judges" — Art 220 says "permanent Judge" — so arguably the bar may not apply to temporary/additional judges (though this is debated).

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** After retirement, a Judge of the Supreme Court:

(a) May practice in the Supreme Court with permission (b) May practice in High Courts other than the one from which they were elevated (c) Cannot plead or act in any court or before any authority in India (d) May practice only in tribunals

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 124(7) imposes an absolute bar — no court, no authority, anywhere in India.

---

**MCQ 2:** The post-retirement bar for a retired High Court Judge under Article 220 is:

(a) Absolute — same as a SC Judge (b) Partial — they can practice in the SC and in other High Courts (c) No bar at all — they can practice anywhere (d) They can practice only in the HC where they served

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 220 bars practice in courts/authorities EXCEPT the SC and other HCs. The bar for SC judges (Art 124(7)) is absolute. This distinction is the key comparison.

---

**MCQ 3:** Which of the following can a retired Supreme Court Judge legally do after retirement?

(a) Argue a case in the Supreme Court (b) Appear before a High Court as an advocate (c) Serve as Chairman of a government commission (d) File a vakalatnama in a District Court

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 124(7) bars pleading/acting in courts and before authorities. It does not bar appointment to commissions, committees, or constitutional posts.

### Revision Points

1. Art 124(7): ABSOLUTE bar — no pleading/acting in any court or before any authority in India.
2. Art 220 (HC judges): PARTIAL bar — can practice in SC and other HCs.
3. The bar is on legal practice — not on post-retirement government/constitutional appointments.
4. Art 220 says "permanent Judge" — debatable whether it covers additional/acting judges.

## Articles 124A–124C — The National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)

### Status: STRUCK DOWN — These articles exist in the Constitution text but are INOPERATIVE.

### Background

The 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, inserted Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C to replace the Collegium system with the NJAC. Parliament also passed the NJAC Act, 2014. Both came into effect on 13 April 2015.

However, in **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)**, a 5-judge Constitution Bench struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act by a 4:1 majority. Justice J. Chelameswar was the lone dissenter.

**Ground:** The NJAC violated the **basic structure** of the Constitution — specifically the **independence of the judiciary** and the **separation of powers**.

### Art 124A — Composition of NJAC (as inserted)

The NJAC was to consist of 6 members:

| Member | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Chief Justice of India | Chairperson, ex officio |
| Two senior-most SC Judges next to CJI | Members, ex officio |
| Union Minister of Law and Justice | Member, ex officio |
| Two eminent persons nominated by a committee of PM + CJI + Leader of Opposition in LS | Members |

**Provisos for eminent persons:**

- One shall be from SC/ST/OBC/Minorities/Women.
- Nominated for 3 years — not eligible for re-nomination.

### Art 124B — Functions of NJAC

The NJAC was to:

1. Recommend persons for appointment as CJI, SC judges, HC Chief Justices, and other HC judges.
2. Recommend transfer of HC CJs and judges.
3. Ensure that recommended persons are of ability and integrity.

### Art 124C — Power of Parliament

Parliament could by law regulate the procedure for appointments, empower the NJAC to lay down regulations, and prescribe the manner of selection.

### Why It Was Struck Down

The SC held that:

1. The inclusion of the Law Minister (executive member) in the appointment process violated judicial independence.
2. The veto power given to any two NJAC members (including the two eminent persons and the Law Minister) could allow the executive to block judicial appointments.
3. The Collegium system, despite its flaws, protected the basic structure (judicial independence) better than the NJAC.
4. The judiciary must have primacy in judicial appointments — this was established by the Second Judges Case (1993) and confirmed by the Third Judges Case (1998).

### Current Position

- Art 124A, 124B, 124C are physically printed in the Constitution — they were INSERTED by the 99th Amendment.
- But they are **inoperative** — the SC declared them unconstitutional.
- The **Collegium system** (CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges for SC appointments) continues to operate.
- The Collegium has no constitutional or statutory basis — it is entirely a judicial creation from the Second and Third Judges Cases.

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "NJAC is the current system" — **FALSE.** Struck down in 2015.

**Trap 2:** "Art 124A has been deleted from the Constitution" — **FALSE.** It exists in the text but is inoperative. The articles were struck down, not repealed.

**Trap 3:** "The NJAC had 5 members" — **FALSE.** It had 6 members (CJI + 2 SC judges + Law Minister + 2 eminent persons).

**Trap 4:** "The NJAC was struck down unanimously" — **FALSE.** 4:1 majority. Justice Chelameswar dissented.

**Trap 5:** "The Collegium is provided for in Art 124A" — **FALSE.** Art 124A provided for the NJAC. The Collegium is a judicial creation with no textual basis.

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** The National Judicial Appointments Commission was inserted by:

(a) Constitution (Ninety-seventh Amendment) Act, 2011 (b) Constitution (Ninety-eighth Amendment) Act, 2012 (c) Constitution (Ninety-ninth Amendment) Act, 2014 (d) Constitution (One Hundredth Amendment) Act, 2015

**Answer:** (c)

---

**MCQ 2:** The NJAC was struck down on the ground that it violated:

**Answer:** (c)

---

**MCQ 3:** The NJAC was to consist of how many members?

(a) 3 (b) 5 (c) 6 (d) 7

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** CJI (1) + 2 senior SC judges (2) + Law Minister (1) + 2 eminent persons (2) = 6.

---

**MCQ 4:** Who was the lone dissenter in the NJAC judgment?

(a) Justice R.M. Lodha (b) Justice J. Chelameswar (c) Justice A.K. Goel (d) Justice Kurian Joseph

**Answer:** (b)

### Revision Points

1. NJAC: 99th Amendment + NJAC Act, 2014 → both struck down in 2015.
2. NJAC composition: 6 members (CJI + 2 SC judges + Law Minister + 2 eminent persons).
3. Struck down for violating basic structure — judicial independence.
4. 4:1 decision — Chelameswar dissented.

6. Collegium restored: CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges.
7. Collegium has NO constitutional or statutory basis — purely judicial creation.

## Article 125 — Salaries, Privileges and Pensions of SC Judges

### Constitutional Text (Summary)

Clause (1): Salaries as determined by **Parliament by law**; until so determined, as per the **Second Schedule**.

Clause (2): Privileges, allowances, leave, and pension as determined by or under law made by **Parliament**; until so determined, as per the **Second Schedule**.

**Proviso to Clause (2):** Neither the privileges nor the allowances of a Judge nor his rights in respect of leave of absence or pension shall be **varied to his disadvantage after his appointment.**

### Analysis

**Who determines salary?** Parliament — by law. Not the President, not the CJI, not the executive. This ensures judicial independence — the executive cannot threaten judges by reducing their salary.

**The non-disadvantage proviso** is critical: once a judge is appointed, their salary, privileges, allowances, leave, and pension cannot be reduced. They can be increased (for future appointees or even current judges), but never decreased for an individual after appointment. This is identical to the protection given to the CAG (Art 148(3)) and the AG.

**Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India:** Under Art 146(3), the administrative expenses of the SC — including ALL salaries, allowances, and pensions of officers and servants — are **charged** upon the CFI (not voted upon by Parliament). This means Parliament cannot refuse to pay them through the budget process. This further protects judicial independence.

### Cross-Connections

**Art 125 → Art 146(3):** Salaries charged on CFI — cannot be voted upon.

**Art 125 → Art 221:** HC judge salaries — also determined by Parliament, also per Second Schedule, same non-disadvantage proviso. But HC expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the STATE.

**Art 125 → Second Schedule:** Part D of the Second Schedule originally specified SC judge salaries. These have been updated by parliamentary enactments.

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** The salaries of Judges of the Supreme Court are determined by:

(a) The President by executive order (b) The Chief Justice of India (c) Parliament by law (d) The Union Cabinet

**Answer:** (c)

---

**MCQ 2:** The salary of a SC Judge after appointment:

(a) Can be increased or decreased by Parliament (b) Can be reduced during a Financial Emergency (c) Cannot be varied to his disadvantage (d) Is fixed by the CJI and cannot be changed

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 125(2) proviso. Note: During a Financial Emergency under Art 360, the President CAN direct reduction of salaries of SC/HC judges — this is the one exception.

---

**MCQ 3:** The administrative expenses of the Supreme Court are:

(a) Voted upon by Parliament annually (b) Charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India (c) Paid from the Contingency Fund of India (d) Borne equally by the Centre and States

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 146(3) — charged, not voted. This protects judicial independence.

### Revision Points

1. Salary: Parliament by law; until then, Second Schedule.
2. Non-disadvantage proviso: cannot reduce after appointment.
3. Exception: Financial Emergency (Art 360) — President can direct salary reduction.
4. SC expenses: charged on CFI (Art 146(3)) — not voted.
5. HC expenses: charged on Consolidated Fund of the State.

## Article 126 — Appointment of Acting Chief Justice

### Constitutional Text

"When the office of Chief Justice of India is vacant or when the Chief Justice is, by reason of absence or otherwise, unable to perform the duties of his office, the duties of the office shall be performed by such one of the other Judges of the Court as the President may appoint for the purpose."

### Analysis

**When does Art 126 apply?**

Two situations:

1. **Office is vacant** — CJI has retired, resigned, been removed, or died, and no successor has been appointed yet.
2. **CJI is unable to perform duties** — absence (on leave, abroad, incapacitated) or "otherwise" (any other reason including illness, disqualification proceedings, etc.).

**Who appoints the Acting CJI?** The **President**. Not the outgoing CJI, not the senior-most judge automatically, not Parliament.

**Convention vs Constitutional text:** By convention, the **senior-most judge** of the SC is appointed as Acting CJI. But the Constitution says "such one of the other Judges as the President may appoint" — theoretically, the President could appoint any SC judge, not necessarily the senior-most. In practice, departure from convention would create a constitutional crisis.

**Duration:** The Acting CJI serves "for the purpose" — meaning until the vacancy is filled or the CJI resumes duties. It is not a permanent appointment.

**Powers:** The Acting CJI has all powers and duties of the CJI — including administrative powers over the SC, assignment of cases, appointment of benches, and the role in the Collegium.

### Comparison: Acting CJI vs Ad Hoc Judge vs Retired Judge Sitting

| Parameter | Acting CJI (Art 126) | Ad Hoc Judge (Art 127) | Retired Judge (Art 128) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | CJI vacancy/absence | No quorum of SC judges | Need for additional judicial capacity |
| Appointed by | President | CJI (with President's consent + HC CJ consultation) | CJI (with President's consent) |
| Who can be appointed | Any other SC Judge | A duly qualified HC Judge | Retired SC/Federal Court Judge or qualified retired HC Judge |
| Status | Full CJI powers | Full SC Judge powers | Full SC Judge powers but NOT deemed a Judge |
| Consent needed from appointee | No (it is an appointment) | Duty to attend (priority over HC duties) | Must consent — voluntary |

### Traps

**Trap 1:** "The senior-most judge automatically becomes Acting CJI" — **NOT CONSTITUTIONALLY MANDATED.** Convention, yes. Constitution says the President appoints.

**Trap 2:** "The Acting CJI has limited powers" — **FALSE.** Full CJI powers including administrative functions.

**Trap 3:** "The CJI nominates his Acting CJI" — **FALSE.** The President appoints under Art 126.

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** When the office of CJI is vacant, the Acting CJI is appointed by:

(a) The senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court automatically (b) The President of India (c) The outgoing Chief Justice (d) The Union Cabinet

**Answer:** (b)

---

**MCQ 2:** Article 126 applies when:

(a) The CJI is on vacation but available on phone (b) The office of CJI is vacant OR the CJI is unable to perform duties (c) The CJI voluntarily delegates work to a senior judge (d) Parliament passes a resolution requesting an Acting CJI

**Answer:** (b)

---

**MCQ 3:** The Acting Chief Justice of India:

(a) Can hear cases but cannot assign benches (b) Has all powers and duties of the CJI (c) Cannot sit on Constitution Benches (d) Can only hear urgent matters

**Answer:** (b)

### Revision Points

1. Art 126: CJI vacant or unable → President appoints Acting CJI.
2. Convention = senior-most judge. Constitution = President's choice.

## Article 127 — Appointment of Ad Hoc Judges

### Constitutional Text (Summary)

Clause (1): If there is no quorum of SC Judges available → CJI may, with **previous consent of the President** and after **consultation with the Chief Justice of the concerned HC**, request in writing a **duly qualified HC Judge** to attend SC sittings as an ad hoc Judge.

Clause (2): It shall be the **duty** of the designated judge, in **priority to other duties**, to attend SC sittings. While attending, he shall have **all jurisdiction, powers and privileges** of a SC Judge.

### Analysis

**Trigger:** No quorum available. This is a rare situation — it means not enough SC judges are available (due to recusals, vacancies, illness, etc.) to constitute the required bench.

**Process:**

1. CJI identifies that no quorum is available.
2. CJI obtains **previous consent of the President** (consent must come BEFORE the request, not after).
3. CJI **consults** with the CJ of the concerned HC (the HC from which the judge will be drawn).
4. CJI requests in **writing** (not orally) a HC judge to sit as ad hoc judge.
5. The HC judge must be **duly qualified for appointment as a SC Judge** (i.e., must satisfy Art 124(3)).

**Duty, not choice:** Once designated, the HC judge MUST attend SC sittings. It is described as a "duty" with "priority to other duties" — meaning their HC work takes second place during this period.

**Powers while sitting:** Complete — "all the jurisdiction, powers and privileges" of a SC Judge. They function as a full SC judge during their ad hoc tenure.

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** Ad hoc Judges of the Supreme Court are appointed under:

(a) Article 126 (b) Article 127 (c) Article 128 (d) Article 224

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 126 = Acting CJI. Art 128 = retired judges sitting. Art 224 = additional/acting HC judges.

---

**MCQ 2:** For appointing an ad hoc Judge, the CJI must obtain:

(a) Approval of Parliament (b) Previous consent of the President and consult the HC Chief Justice (c) Recommendation of the Collegium (d) Permission of the Attorney General

**Answer:** (b)

---

**MCQ 3:** An HC Judge sitting as an ad hoc Judge of the SC:

(a) Has limited jurisdiction (b) Can only hear civil matters (c) Has all jurisdiction, powers and privileges of a SC Judge (d) Cannot deliver judgments

**Answer:** (c)

### Revision Points

1. Art 127: no quorum → CJI + President's consent + HC CJ consultation → HC judge sits as ad hoc.
2. HC judge must be duly qualified for SC appointment (Art 124(3)).
3. Request must be in writing.
4. Duty to attend — priority over HC work.
5. Full SC judge powers while sitting.

## Article 128 — Attendance of Retired Judges at SC Sittings

### Constitutional Text (Summary)

CJI may, with **previous consent of the President**, request any person who has held office as a Judge of the SC or Federal Court (or a duly qualified retired HC Judge) to sit and act as a SC Judge.

Such person shall be entitled to allowances as the **President may by order determine** and shall have **all the jurisdiction, powers and privileges** of a SC Judge but **shall not otherwise be deemed to be a Judge** of the Court.

Proviso: No such person shall be required to sit unless **he consents** to do so.

### Analysis

**Who can be called?**

1. Retired SC Judges.
2. Retired Federal Court Judges (pre-1950 — historically relevant).
3. Retired HC Judges who are duly qualified for SC appointment (added by 15th Amendment, 1963).

**Key features:**

- **Voluntary:** The retired judge must consent. Unlike an ad hoc judge (Art 127), who has a duty to attend, a retired judge under Art 128 can refuse.
- **Not deemed a SC Judge:** This is critical. The retired judge has all powers WHILE sitting, but is not counted in the SC's strength, is not part of the Collegium, and does not have the administrative status of a regular SC judge.
- **Allowance by President:** The President determines the allowance — it is not the same as a regular SC judge's salary.
- **CJI initiates + President consents:** Same pattern as Art 127.

### Comparison Summary: Art 126 vs 127 vs 128

| Feature | Art 126 (Acting CJI) | Art 127 (Ad Hoc) | Art 128 (Retired Judge) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Person called | Sitting SC Judge | Sitting HC Judge | Retired SC/Federal Court/HC Judge |
| Who decides | President | CJI + President's consent | CJI + President's consent |
| Consent of person | Not required | Not required (duty) | REQUIRED (voluntary) |
| Status | Full CJI | Full SC Judge | Powers of SC Judge but NOT deemed a Judge |
| Counted in strength | Yes | Yes (while sitting) | No |

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** Under Article 128, a retired Judge sitting in the SC:

(a) Is deemed to be a Judge of the Supreme Court (b) Has jurisdiction and powers but is not deemed to be a Judge of the Court (c) Can only hear advisory reference cases (d) Has limited powers as prescribed by the CJI

**Answer:** (b)

---

**MCQ 2:** A retired Judge invited under Art 128 to sit in the SC:

(a) Must attend — it is a constitutional duty (b) Can decline — consent is required (c) Can attend only during vacation (d) Must be below 70 years of age

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Proviso — "nothing in this article shall be deemed to require any such person to sit and act unless he consents so to do." This is voluntary, unlike Art 127.

---

**MCQ 3:** The allowance payable to a retired Judge sitting under Art 128 is determined by:

(a) The Chief Justice of India (b) Parliament by law (c) The President by order (d) The Registrar of the Supreme Court

**Answer:** (c)

### Revision Points

1. Art 128: retired judges sit → CJI + President's consent.
2. Voluntary — retired judge can refuse.
3. Full powers WHILE sitting — but NOT deemed a Judge.
4. Not counted in SC strength.
5. Allowance: President determines by order.

## Article 129 — Supreme Court as Court of Record

### Constitutional Text

"The Supreme Court shall be a court of record and shall have all the powers of such a court including the power to punish for contempt of itself."

### Analysis

**What is a Court of Record?**

A court of record is a court whose proceedings are recorded, whose records are admissible as evidence, and which has the power to punish for contempt. The concept comes from English common law.

**Implications:**

1. **Records as evidence:** The judgments, decrees, and orders of the SC are preserved and can be cited as evidence. They cannot be questioned or challenged in any other court (except by review before the SC itself under Art 137).
2. **Precedential value:** As a court of record, SC decisions have binding force under Art 141 (law declared by SC is binding on all courts).
3. **Contempt power:** The SC can punish for contempt of **itself**. This is both civil contempt (willful disobedience of court orders) and criminal contempt (acts that scandalize or lower the authority of the court).

**The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971:**

- Parliament enacted this Act under its legislative power.
- The Act defines civil contempt and criminal contempt.
- However, the SC's contempt power under Art 129 is a **constitutional power** — it exists independently of the 1971 Act.
- The Act cannot limit the SC's constitutional contempt power, though it can regulate procedures.
- Art 142(2) also gives the SC contempt power — these two provisions (Art 129 and Art 142(2)) are complementary.

**Comparison with HC:**

| Parameter | Supreme Court (Art 129) | High Court (Art 215) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Court of record | Yes | Yes |
| Contempt power | Can punish for contempt of itself | Can punish for contempt of itself |
| Binding on | All courts in India (Art 141) | Subordinate courts within its jurisdiction |
| Contempt of subordinate courts | Art 129 covers contempt of SC only; but Art 142(2) + Contempt of Courts Act gives broader powers | Art 215 covers contempt of HC; also has power over subordinate courts' contempt |

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** Under Article 129, the Supreme Court is:

(a) A court of appeal only (b) A court of record with power to punish for contempt of itself (c) A court that can punish for contempt of any court (d) A court established by Parliament

**Answer:** (b)

---

**MCQ 2:** The contempt power of the Supreme Court under Article 129 is:

(a) Derived from the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (b) A constitutional power independent of any statute (c) Limited to civil contempt only (d) Exercisable only with the consent of the Attorney General

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 129 is a constitutional provision. The 1971 Act regulates procedure but does not create the SC's contempt power.

---

**MCQ 3:** Which Article declares that High Courts are also courts of record?

(a) Article 214 (b) Article 215 (c) Article 226 (d) Article 227

**Answer:** (b)

### Revision Points

1. Art 129: SC = court of record → records are evidence + contempt power.
2. Contempt power is constitutional — independent of the 1971 Act.
3. Art 215: HC also a court of record with contempt power.
4. SC contempt = of itself. Broader contempt powers through Art 142(2) + 1971 Act.

## Article 130 — Seat of the Supreme Court

### Constitutional Text

"The Supreme Court shall sit in Delhi or in such other place or places, as the Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, from time to time, appoint."

### Analysis

**Default seat:** Delhi. This is constitutionally mandated. The SC sits in Delhi unless it is moved.

**Who can change the seat?** The **CJI** — with the **approval of the President**. Not Parliament, not the Union Cabinet, not the Law Minister.

**"From time to time":** The CJI can appoint different places at different times. Theoretically, the SC could sit in different cities for different periods — like a circuit bench system. However, this power has never been exercised. The SC has always sat in Delhi.

**"Place or places":** The CJI can designate one or more places. This suggests the possibility of SC benches in multiple cities simultaneously — a concept that has been debated politically but never implemented.

**The SC Bench Outside Delhi Debate:**

- There has been a long-standing demand for SC benches in other regions (Chennai, Mumbai, Kolkata) to improve access to justice.
- In 2010, a Constitution Bench considered this issue in V. Vasanthakumar v. H.C. Bhatia and observed that establishing benches outside Delhi is within the CJI's power under Art 130.
- However, the SC has consistently resisted this idea, citing administrative difficulties and the importance of having a single apex court.
- As of 2026, the SC sits only in Delhi.

### MCQs

**MCQ 1:** The seat of the Supreme Court is:

(a) Determined by Parliament by law (b) Delhi, as specified in the Constitution (c) Decided by the President without any consultation (d) Wherever the Chief Justice directs, without any approval

**Answer:** (b) **Explanation:** Art 130 — "shall sit in Delhi." The CJI can change it with President's approval, but the default is constitutionally fixed as Delhi.

---

**MCQ 2:** The seat of the Supreme Court can be changed from Delhi by:

(a) A constitutional amendment (b) An Act of Parliament (c) The Chief Justice of India with the approval of the President (d) The Union Cabinet by resolution

**Answer:** (c) **Explanation:** Art 130 — CJI + President's approval. No legislation or amendment needed.

---

**MCQ 3:** Under Article 130, the SC can sit in:

(a) Delhi only — no other place (b) Delhi or such other place or places as the CJI may appoint with President's approval (c) Any State capital as determined by Parliament (d) Delhi and one other city designated by the President

**Answer:** (b)

### Revision Points

1. Art 130: default seat = Delhi.
2. CJI can change with President's approval.
3. "Place or places" — multiple locations possible in theory.
4. Never exercised — SC has always sat in Delhi.
5. Not Parliament, not Cabinet — only CJI + President.
